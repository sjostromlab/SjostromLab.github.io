Take-home messages

  • Fast voltage signals are filtered most. The electrode cannot follow a spike as faithfully as a slow membrane response.
  • Injected current creates an I·Rs voltage error. It displaces the apparent spike threshold and the current-step response.
  • Bridge balance removes an offset, not lost bandwidth. It cannot reconstruct rapid waveform components already filtered out.
  • Slow EPSPs are largely preserved. They change slowly and do not pass the stimulating current through the recording electrode.

The shared membrane and electrode models

The same membrane controls apply to all three demonstrations. Rm is the passive leak resistance, Cm is the total membrane capacitance, and Vrest sets the initial equilibrium. In the Hodgkin–Huxley model, the leak reversal potential is recalculated so that the selected Vrest remains stable without an unreported holding current. Because voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels also contribute near rest, the HH cell's measured small-signal input resistance need not equal the displayed passive Rm exactly.

The same electrode is also used in all three demonstrations. Series resistance Rs and residual pipette capacitance Cp give an electrode time constant τelectrode = RsCp. During electrode current injection, the measured voltage approaches Vm + IinjRs. Bridge balance subtracts the selected fraction of this resistive voltage error. Cp neutralization reduces the effective pipette capacitance and therefore shortens τelectrode.

Reading about bridge balance

Rossant et al. (2012) describe conventional bridge balance in the Abstract and Introduction and illustrate its onset and offset artifacts in Figure 1. Bridge balance subtracts the estimated steady resistive drop, IinjRs; it does not reverse electrode low-pass filtering. Because a real electrode responds with a finite time constant, instantaneous bridge subtraction can itself create brief transients when current begins or ends. At current onset, for example, the bridge immediately subtracts IinjRs before the actual electrode voltage has fully developed, producing the downward transient seen here.

1. Passive step: two exponential components

A current step of the selected amplitude and duration drives an isopotential passive membrane with τm = RmCm. The recording contains the slower membrane response plus the faster electrode component governed by RsCp. Compare the raw voltage offset with the bridge-balanced error, and calculate IinjRs from the displayed settings.

2. Action potential: voltage displacement and filtering

A current step elicits a Hodgkin–Huxley action potential during a 30-ms simulation. The selected Rm, Cm, and Vrest now determine the spike model's passive leak, capacitance, and resting equilibrium as well as the other two protocols. Rs produces an IinjRs displacement that makes the apparent spike threshold more positive. The finite electrode bandwidth also reduces maximum dV/dt and alters spike peak and half-width. Bridge balance can correct the resistive displacement, but it cannot reconstruct rapid waveform components already lost to low-pass filtering.

3. EPSP: the slow-event comparison

The EPSP is generated by a normalized double-exponential, AMPA-like conductance. Rise τ and decay τ control its kinetics independently; they are exponential time constants rather than a direct 10–90% rise-time measurement. The synaptic current crosses the membrane rather than being injected through the recording electrode, so there is no IinjRs displacement. When the EPSP changes slowly relative to τelectrode, its peak and timing are therefore recorded with very little error.

Suggested exercises

  1. Set Rs to 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 MΩ. In Compare all, contrast the passive voltage offset, spike errors, and EPSP peak error.
  2. Calculate IinjRs for both current-step protocols and compare your answers with the displayed voltage displacements.
  3. Calculate τm and τelectrode. Use their relative sizes to predict which signals should be distorted most.
  4. Vary Rm, Cm, and Vrest. Compare their effects on the passive response, EPSP, and spike threshold or latency.
  5. Set bridge balance to 100%, then vary Cp. Which spike errors remain even when the resistive displacement is corrected?
  6. Increase Cp neutralization and determine when the spike becomes acceptably accurate.
  7. Shorten the EPSP rise and decay constants. At what kinetics does electrode filtering become appreciable?

Scope and references

The passive response and EPSP use a single isopotential membrane. The spike uses the classic Hodgkin–Huxley equations with fixed maximum Na+ and K+ conductances, while the shared controls set its passive leak resistance, total capacitance, and resting equilibrium. The electrode is represented by one resistance and one residual capacitance; real electrodes can have several time constants and additional nonlinearities. The teaching comparison follows the supplied series-resistance simulation. Bridge balance and capacitive electrode errors are discussed by Rossant et al. (2012), A calibration-free electrode compensation method. The action-potential model follows Hodgkin & Huxley (1952).
