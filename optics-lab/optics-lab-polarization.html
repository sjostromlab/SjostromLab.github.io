How the optics work

A linear polarizer transmits the component of the electric field along its transmission axis. For a linearly polarized input, rotating that axis changes the transmitted power according to Malus’s law. The transmitted field lies along the filter axis. The beam still travels in its original direction in this ideal model.

Rotating filter: Pout / Pin = cos²(φ − θ)
Half-wave plate: θafter = 2α − θ (modulo 180°)
Fixed PBS transmitted port: PT / Pin = cos²(2α − θ)
Fixed PBS reflected port: PR / Pin = sin²(2α − θ)

The wave plate rotates the incident linear polarization without attenuating it in this ideal model. The fixed PBS then separates it into two fixed, orthogonal output polarizations. Changing α changes how much power goes to each port. The transmitted beam follows the straight path; the reflected beam leaves at 90°. Those paths do not move when the wave plate rotates.

A fixed PBS alone is not an adjustable attenuator for a fixed input polarization. The half-wave plate supplies the adjustment. Rotating the PBS itself would change its polarization basis and can change the optical geometry.

Assumptions and terminology

All powers are time-averaged. The polarizer is modelled as an ideal absorptive filter; the rejected fraction is labelled absorbed. Real polarizers can reject light by other mechanisms. The half-wave plate is ideal at its operating wavelength and the PBS is lossless with perfect extinction. Real losses, finite extinction, wavelength dependence, beam walk-off and coatings are not included.

P and S are defined relative to the cube’s plane of incidence, not universally relative to the room. In the illustrated horizontal bench plane, P is in that plane and S is perpendicular to it. Output field plots use local transverse coordinates. At zero power no transmitted polarization can be measured; the display labels only the port or filter axis.

The introductory circular and elliptical examples are fully polarized. Unpolarized light is a different case and is not modelled here. End-on drawings represent electric-field motion, not a mechanical motion of the beam.
